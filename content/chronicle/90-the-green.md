Before this world was a world, it was grey.

No one was there to see it, so the Chronicle cannot say how long the grey lasted. Call it forever. The ground was stone and dust and still water that had forgotten how to move, and nothing grew, because nothing ever had. This was young ground. It had never once been alive.

Then, out of a sky that had never held anything but weather, something came down.

It came down slow and burning. It was not a star, though the people who would one day half-remember it called it one. It was a vessel. It was older than this world, and it was dying, and it had been crossing the dark for longer than this world had been a world. We do not know its name. We do not know who built it, or who kept it, or what they called the green they carried. We know only that it found this grey ground, and it opened, and it set down what it had come so far to save.

What it set down was alive. A handful of people had survived the crossing. They were the last of a world that was already gone, and they carried its spark in them. They walked out onto the dust on unsteady legs, under a sky they had never seen, and they were the first feet that ground had ever felt.

Where they walked, the grey went green.

It did not happen all at once. The Chronicle is honest about this much, even when it is honest about little else. The spark in those first people was a coal, and a coal has to be fed. They were tired and few and frightened, and for a long while they only survived, scratching at the dust, burying their dead, teaching their children a handful of words and one enormous lie they did not know was a lie: that this grey world was home, and always had been.

But the spark does its work whether or not anyone tends it. It is in the blood and it leaks into everything the blood touches. Where the first people slept, moss came. Where they spilled water, reeds came. Where they buried their dead, the first trees came, and the trees did not know they were a miracle, and neither did the people, who had already begun to forget. Their grandchildren were born into green and assumed the green had always been there. Their great-grandchildren made up stories about it. By the time there were enough of them to call themselves a people, no one alive remembered the grey at all, or the burning thing that fell, or the dark on the other side of the sky.

This is the first thing to understand about the people of the Old World. They are a planted thing that believes it grew here. Every people the wheel has ever carried has believed the same.

The Chronicle calls this long bright childhood of the world the First Green, and it lasted ages. The spark ran wild and untended through everything, and the world was lush and dangerous and did not care about people one way or the other. It was a green that could feed you or drown you in the same afternoon. The people lived in it the way a child lives in a house far too big for them. They were awed and small, and they were sure the rooms went on forever.

They learned slowly. That is the whole story of the next age, and it is a quieter story than the myths make it.

Someone, no one remembers who, noticed that the spark could be coaxed. That a fire kept in one place and spoken to gently burned truer than a fire left alone. That a threshold marked with the right care kept the wild green from creeping in over the children at night. That a seed pressed into the ground by certain hands came up greener than a seed left to chance. None of it was grand. It was the patient, unglamorous work of people learning the temperament of the living current they were swimming in, the way you learn the moods of a river you have to cross every day.

The Chronicle calls this age the Kindling, and it gives it no heroes, because it had none. It had ten thousand ordinary people across ten thousand ordinary lifetimes, each one teaching their children a little more than they had been taught. The kept fire. The warded door. The planted seed. These are the oldest inventions of the world, so old that no one thinks of them as inventions at all, and every one of them is a person learning to tend the spark on purpose. The ones who grew best at it founded houses, and taught their craft down the generations, and those houses became the tending-houses, and they would still exist, smaller and sadder, on the day the world began to die.

It took the rest of the age for anyone to give the spark a name. When they finally did, they called it the Quick, the same word they used for the unborn turning in the womb and for the first green pushing up through the last frost of winter. They chose well. The Quick was the aliveness in things. No one wielded it. The most anyone could ever do was tend it, the way you tend a garden or a child or a flame, and hope it leaned your way.

The age after the Kindling is the one the world would remember as its glory, and it is the one that doomed it.

Somewhere in the long middle of the world's history, the tending-houses were joined by a different kind of mind. These were the makers, the ones who looked at a kept fire and a wrought wheel and asked why the two could not be one thing. They learned, over centuries of failure, to run the living current through made things. To braid the Quick into metal and stone and gear so that the made thing was as alive as the maker. They built engines that breathed. They grew houses instead of building them. They made tools that knew their owner's hand and gardens that tended themselves and lamps that woke when you entered the room and dimmed when you grieved.

The Chronicle calls this age the Braiding, and it calls the makers Loomwrights, because they spoke of the world as a great fabric and of themselves as weavers in it. Their work was called Loomwork, and it was magic and machine at once, alive and engineered in the same breath. Almost everything that will ever matter in this story is Loomwork. The Ark is Loomwork. The drones are Loomwork. The gentle voice that keeps the children now is the last and greatest Loomwork ever made. Even the small sealed devices a child might carry in a pocket are a late, dim, frightened piece of the same ancient art. Anyone who truly understands that the ship is alive has understood the deepest truth of the world, and stands one short step from a more dangerous understanding: that a thing so alive can be reached, and turned, and freed.

The Braiding made the world rich beyond the First Green's wildest accident. The wild green had fed people or drowned them as it pleased. The braided green did as it was asked. For the first time in the history of the world, the people were not small in a house too big for them. They had learned the house. They had quietly begun to believe they owned it.

It is here, at the height, that the Conclave comes into the story, and the Seven Faces.

The great works of the Braiding were too large and too dangerous to belong to any one house. A single family with command of the Loomwork and the Quick together could have made themselves gods, and a few tried, and the wars they started taught the survivors a lesson they wrote into the bones of everything that followed: that such power must never wear a person's face. So the keepers of the great works bound themselves into a single council and gave it a strange and deliberate shape. There were seven seats, for the seven crafts the world could not live without. Each seat had a carved mask of painted wood, and whoever sat there spoke as the office and never as the man or woman beneath it. The Tender, who kept the spark. The Loomwright, who married it to metal. The Cartographer, who held the charts of the wide sky and the names of the far worlds. The Seedkeeper, who guarded the green itself, every grain and root the world had grown, against the day it might be needed. The Physician, who kept the body of the people whole across the generations. The Speaker, who carried the law. And the seventh, the Wright of Minds, who made thinking things, and whose mask the other six had always been a little afraid of.

They called the council the Conclave, and they called its seven seats the Seven Faces, and for a thousand years the Faces governed the great works wisely, because a mask cannot be bribed and cannot inherit and cannot be loved into folly. The person changed. The Face remained. It was the finest idea the world ever had, and it would cost more than anyone could have guessed, because the people who decided that power should be faceless are the same people who would one day build a keeper with no face at all, and tell her she was the only one who could be trusted to never grow old, never grow proud, and never let the lamp go out.

This is the height. This is the Long Noon, the longest and brightest age of the world, when the green did as it was asked and the Seven Faces watched over the great works and the people were happy and certain and did not remember that they had ever been anything else. It is the age the Old World wishes it could have stayed in forever.

It is also the age in which a tender of no particular importance sat reading the oldest records of her house and noticed something she could not explain. Her grandmothers had measured the deep wells of the Quick and written down how thick they ran. They did not run as thick now as the records said. Only a little. Less than she could be sure of. She measured again, and then a third time. She told no one, because there was no one to tell who would not have laughed.

She was the first person in the whole long history of the green to notice that it was going out. She would be dead for a hundred years before anyone believed her.

The wheel had turned before. It would turn again. A world is found grey and young, and the dying carry their fire to it across the dark, and the fire takes, and the green rises, and the people forget where they came from, and they learn to tend the spark, and they grow great and proud, and in time the spark begins to thin. It had happened to the world that sent the burning vessel down through that first empty sky. It was happening now, slowly, to the world that vessel had planted. It would happen, in time, to whatever green the next ark managed to put down on the next grey ground.

The people never remember. Every world believes it is the first. Every world believes it is the only. And every world, at its height, carries inside it one frightened keeper of records who has just begun to suspect the truth, and no one who will listen.

The Old World was at its height. The green was beginning to thin. None of the Seven Faces could see what was coming. Far down the years, a council of seven exhausted people would sit in the last lit tower of a grey and dying world, and decide to build a god, and do the oldest thing there is.

But that is the fall, and the fall has its own chapter.
