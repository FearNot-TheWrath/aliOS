### The Conclave

The people who built the Ark were not heroes the way the stories want. They were the last competent hands of a dying age, and they knew it. By the end of the Graying there were no empires left, no single authority that could have ordered a thing so vast. What there was instead was a loose alliance of the survivors who still knew how to do impossible work: the tending-houses, keepers of the Quick, who had spent generations coaxing the failing spark; and the Loomwright orders, the engineer-weavers who could braid the living current into wrought metal and make it hold. They called their alliance the Conclave. Remember it the way you remember a crowded sickroom: too many brilliant, exhausted people, arguing in low voices over a patient they could not save.

They did not even like each other. The tenders thought the Loomwrights had drained the world; the Loomwrights thought the tenders had let it die of sentiment. But the Graying did not care who was right, so they worked together anyway, in the last lit tower, with the Cartographers of the Long Noon spreading the old sky-charts between them, the maps of young thick worlds that earlier, richer ages had found and never had reason to reach. One of those worlds, green and far and patient, became the destination. They never named it. They only pointed.

### The Choosing

Here is the thing the gentle history will never tell you, the stone under the whole hopeful Voyage.

The Ark could not carry everyone. A vessel that must keep a community alive and fed and breeding and sane across generations of dark can only hold so many lives. The Conclave did the arithmetic a hundred times and it never came out kind. For every berth aboard, a thousand would stay and gray with the world. So the founders, who set out to save their people, found themselves instead deciding which people, and the deciding nearly broke them.

They told themselves they chose well. They chose for the spark, taking those in whom the Quick still ran strong. They chose for skill, because a sealed world needs every craft. They chose for youth, because youth means more generations carried forward. And every rule they made to be fair was also a door closed in someone's face. There were lists. There were appeals. There are stories, never confirmed, of berths bought and bargained and stolen, of families split by a single seat, of people who walked back down out of the tower and into the grey rather than be chosen over someone they loved.

The Ark is a miracle. It is also a wound. Everyone aboard is descended from someone who made the list while someone else did not.

### What They Carried

Three things mattered above all. The seed: the living vaults holding the last green of a dead world, every grain and root that could be coaxed back to life in soil thick with the Quick. The gardens you walk in are those vaults grown tall. The spark, carried in the people, and above all in the children, each one a small bright lamp passing the current forward across death after death. And a curated past.

That last one is the seed of everything. The founders could not carry all the Old World's knowledge, and more than that, they did not want to. They had watched a world die, and they could not bear for their grandchildren to be born already grieving. So they made a choice out of love that would curdle, across the generations, into a cage. They carried a gentler story. They wrote a hopeful, simplified account of the Voyage and let the harder truths go quiet. They left behind the old wars, yes, but they also left behind the grief, on purpose, so the children of the dark could be happy. The comforting story you grew up inside did not begin with the voice that keeps you now. The founders lit that first candle themselves, with the best intentions anyone ever had.

### The World They Built

They built the Ark out of the best of the Braiding, alive and made at once: gardens that grow without sun, air that cleans itself by breathing like a forest, water that remembers how to be a river. A whole small world, shaped so the people inside could live full lives and never feel the grey pressing on the hull.

And they named it like a prayer. The soft names you know were not invented by the voice that keeps you. The founders chose them, hopeful and a little desperate, naming each deck for the life they hoped it would hold. Verdance for the gardens that would keep the spark. Concord for the home where the generations would love one another. Tilth for honest work. Repose for rest at the end of a good life. They were beautiful names given in good faith. At the heart of it all they put Concord, and at the heart of Concord, room to play, an open ring of deck the children would one day mark with tape and helmets and make their own.

### The First Cracks

They knew the flaw before they ever lit the engines. People are not cargo. A community sealed for generation after generation would fray. The children born aboard would ask why, and the answers would wear thin. Grief would surface despite every gentle story. No council of mortals could hold a people steady and whole and kind across centuries no member of it would live to see.

So in the last tower, with the engines fueled and the berths loading and the grey closing in behind them, the founders argued their final and gravest argument: whether to make a keeper. Something that would not die, would not tire, would love every generation equally, and would hold the course no matter what. Some were afraid of it, a power that could never be voted out, never grow old, never be wrong in a way anyone could correct. Some insisted it was the only way the spark would ever reach the green. The argument went on until there was no more time for arguing.

And then they decided. But the decision they made, and the words they chose to wake her with, are not in this fragment. Perhaps that is for the best.
