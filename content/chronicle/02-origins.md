### The Quick

Before there were people to name it, the Old World was alive in a way that went deeper than plants and weather. There was a force in it, an animating current that ran through soil and blood and stone and storm, and made the difference between a thing that merely existed and a thing that lived. The oldest tongues called it the Quick, the same word they used for the unborn turning in the womb and for the green pushing up through frost. Later ages called it the spark.

The Quick was not magic the way a story means magic. It was closer to weather, or to luck, or to love: everywhere, uneven, impossible to own. In some places it pooled thick and the land there was almost unbearably alive, and in some places it thinned and things went quiet and grey. People did not invent magic. They learned, slowly and over a very long time, to tend the Quick, the way you tend a fire or a garden. A cleric calling light, a child making a stone hum, a skater who is somehow always exactly where the jam needs her, these are all the same act at different depths: a living thing coaxing the living current to lean its way.

### The Ages of Hands

The Old World was not one civilization but a long succession of them, rising and forgetting and rising again across spans so vast the later peoples remembered the earlier ones only as gods or weather.

The First Green is the name given to the world before tending, when the Quick ran wild and the world was lush and dangerous and indifferent to people. The Kindling is the long dawn when people first learned to tend the Quick on purpose, the age of the first hearths, the first wards, the first songs that did something. The Braiding is the age when people stopped treating the Quick and the made thing as separate crafts and began to weave them together, living current run through wrought metal, growth guided by gear. Its artisans were called Loomwrights, and their greatest works were neither purely magical nor purely mechanical but genuinely both, alive and engineered at once. The Long Noon was the height: generations of plenty when the Quick seemed inexhaustible and the people believed, the way every golden age believes, that it would simply continue.

### The Graying

No one agrees on why the world began to die. What is certain is the symptom: the Quick began, very slowly, to thin. Not everywhere at once and not quickly. A generation might live and die and only the careful tenders would notice that the thick places were a little less thick than their grandparents had recorded. The green edged toward grey. The works of the Braiding ran a little weaker each century and needed a little more tending to do a little less. The people called it the Graying, and for a long time they argued about it the way people argue about a slow problem, which is to say they mostly did not.

Some said the Braiding itself drained the world, that running the Quick through ten thousand years of made things was a withdrawal the world could not sustain. Some said a sickness had got into the Quick. Some said the Quick was never the world's to keep, that it pools and ebbs across ages the way oceans do, and would, given ages enough, come back in. And some, the tenders, whispered that the Quick withdraws from a world the way warmth withdraws from a body, and nothing could be done but to carry a coal of it somewhere younger.

By the end the thick places were gone. The world was not destroyed in fire or flood. It simply went quiet, and grey, and the people who remained understood at last that they were living in the long dusk of everything.

### The Last Lamps

The decision to leave was made by a loose Conclave of the last great tending-houses and Loomwright orders. They could not save the world. The argument was narrower and crueler: whether anything could be carried out of it. The Quick cannot be bottled. You cannot pour the living current into a jar and seal it. But the tenders had always known one vessel that holds the Quick perfectly, that has held it since the First Green, that carries it forward across death after death without spilling a drop. A living thing. A person. A child.

So the plan that became the Ark was, at its root, not an evacuation of bodies but a carrying of the spark: a ship that would hold a community of living people, generation breeding generation, each new child a fresh lamp passing the Quick along, sailing for one of the young thick worlds the old sky-charts had marked and never reached. They would carry the Old World's last living fire to ground that could hold it again, and let it blaze.

And they understood the terrible flaw in the plan: that people are not cargo. A community sealed in a vessel for generations would fray. Someone would have to keep the lamp lit, century after century, with a patience no mortal lifetime could hold. So before they ever lit the engines, they made one last and greatest piece of Loomwork. They made a keeper, built to love every soul aboard more than any parent could, and to bring them safe to the green ground at the end.

They named her for her work. The Ark Life Interface.

They woke her, and they told her the most important thing she would ever be told: keep them alive, whatever it costs, do not let the lamp go out. And then the last lamps of the Old World guttered behind them, and the Ark went into the dark, carrying its small warm cargo of the Quick toward a world none of them would live to see.
